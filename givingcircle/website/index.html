What is a website?

A website is simply a collection of files, namely HTML and CSS. When you type its address or tap a link, your browser asks the computer that's hosting those files for one of them, usually one called index.html, and turns it into the page you see.

Most of those files are plain text you could open in any text editor. HTML says what's on the page: a heading, a paragraph, a picture, a link. CSS says how it looks: the colours, the type, the spacing, where things sit. Pictures, sounds and fonts are files in the same folder. Links join a page to other pages, on your own site and on anyone else's, and all those links together are what make it a web.

That's all a website has to be. Anyone can make one with simple tools, and they can look like anything!

Poetic computation

Most of what computers do is meant to be useful: feeds, forms, shops. Poetic computation is the idea that code can also be a material, like paint or clay or words, that you use to make something personal, strange or beautiful. The School for Poetic Computation, an artist-run school started in New York in 2013, teaches code this way: more like creative writing than a job skill.

A website is a good place to start, because when you make one you're both its author and its architect. It doesn't have to be a résumé or a store. It can be a room, a shelf, a garden, a diary, a zine, or a place to keep things so they don't disappear. Laurel Schwulst's essay My website is a shifting house next to a river of knowledge. What could yours be? is a good way to start imagining yours. She calls a website “an individual act of collective ambition.”

HTML is the web's oldest material and its most forgiving one. You don't need to install anything, buy anything or ask anyone's permission to use it.

What we will learn

The hope is that after reading through and following this workshop, you'll be able to make a small website, keep it, and add to it a little at a time, until it becomes a living archive of whatever you'd like: your work, your days, the things you love. Just what you choose to keep. In the future we can learn how to host these files so anyone on the web can access them, but for now they can be passed around as files through message, email, flash drive, or any other way of sending files.

The first session

  1. Watch the two videos.

  2. Open the plain text editor that's already on your computer.

    • Mac: TextEdit. Open its Settings first. Under New Document, choose Plain text. Under Open and Save, tick Display HTML files as HTML code instead of formatted text, and untick Add “.txt” extension to plain text files.
    • Windows: Notepad. When you save, set Save as type to All files, so your file stays .html.

  3. Download the starter folder my-website.zip

    Unzip it and you'll have a folder called my-website with an index.html inside. Or make it yourself: make a folder called my-website, and inside it save a new file called index.html with this in it: 

    <!DOCTYPE html>
<html>
  <head>
    <meta charset="utf-8">
    <meta name="viewport"
          content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1">
    <title>my website</title>
  </head>
  <body>
    <h1>hello, i'm …</h1>
    <p>this is my website. it's a room i'm building.</p>
  </body>
</html>

  4. Open index.html in your web browser: drag it onto a browser window, or double-click it. That page is your website. For now it lives only on your computer.

  5. After the CSS video, give it some style. Put this inside <head>, just before </head>:

    <style>
  body {
    background: lavender;
    font-family: Georgia, serif;
    padding: 2em;
  }
</style>

  6. Change one thing. Save. Reload the browser. That loop, change, save, reload, is most of what making websites is.

Adding sound and video

Songs, recordings, voice notes and videos live on your website the same way everything else does: as files in your folder.

1. Put the file in your folder

Copy it into my-website, next to index.html. Give it a short name with no spaces or capital letters, like my-song.mp3. A name that works on your computer can break once it's online if it has either. MP3 for sound and MP4 for video play in every browser. Videos straight off a phone can be huge, so export a smaller copy if you can.

2. Link to it

The simplest way is a link. Clicking it opens the file in the browser's own player.

<a href="my-song.mp3">listen to my song</a>

3. Or put a player on the page

<audio src="my-song.mp3" controls></audio>

<video src="my-video.mp4" controls width="480"></video>

controls gives you the play button and the timeline. Leave it out and there's no way to press play. width sets how wide the video is, in pixels. To show a still picture before it plays, add poster="still.jpg".

For a video that plays by itself, over and over, like a moving picture:

<video src="loop.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline></video>

Browsers stop sound from starting on its own, so a video can only play by itself when it's muted. playsinline keeps iPhones from jumping to full screen.

4. If it's inside another folder

If you keep your sound and video in a folder inside my-website, say one called media, put the folder's name in front: src="media/my-song.mp3".

This page does exactly that. Its soundscape and its video live in its own media folder:

<audio src="media/rainbirdssoundscape.mp3"
       controls preload="none"></audio>
<video src="media/oregonoceanvideo.mp4"
       poster="media/oregonoceanvideo.jpg"
       controls playsinline preload="none"></video>

preload="none" means the file only downloads when someone presses play, which saves data on phones. It's worth adding to anything long. Because nothing has downloaded yet, the video shows its poster, a still picture saved next to it, until then.

A video straight off a phone often carries the exact place it was filmed, hidden inside the file. This one was made smaller and had that taken out before it went on the page. Do the same with yours: ask us how.

5. Video that's already on YouTube or Vimeo

Press Share, then Embed, copy the code it gives you (it starts with <iframe) and paste it into your page. If you see privacy-enhanced mode, tick it. That player comes from their website, not your folder. If you open your page straight from your computer, YouTube may show Error 153 instead of the video. That's normal, and it plays once your site is online.

Laurel's two videos at the top of The first session are embedded this way. Here's the first one:

<iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/CkzbI1Tv_rQ"
        title="Basics of HTML, by Laurel Schwulst"
        allow="encrypted-media; picture-in-picture; fullscreen"
        allowfullscreen
        referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin">
</iframe>

Keeping it

Your website is the my-website folder. Everything is in it, your sound and video too. Keep it somewhere you'll find it again, and keep a copy too: a USB stick, your cloud drive, or an email to yourself. Bring it every time.

This page is made the same way yours will be. On a computer, right-click it and choose View Page Source: it's the same HTML you're about to write, just more of it.
