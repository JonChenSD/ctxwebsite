What is a website?

A website is simply a collection of files, namely HTML and CSS. When you type its address or tap a link, your browser asks the computer that's hosting those files for one of them, usually one called index.html, and turns it into the page you see.

Most of those files are plain text you can open in any text editor. HTML says what's on the page: a heading, a paragraph, a picture, a link. CSS says how it looks: the colors, the type, the spacing, and where things sit. Pictures, sounds and fonts are also just files in the same folder. Links connect a page to other pages, on your own site and on anyone else's, and all those links together are what make it a web.

That's all a website has to be. Anyone can make one with simple tools, and they can look like anything.

Poetic computation

Most of what computers do is made to be useful, like feeds, forms and online shops. Poetic computation is the idea that code can also be a material, like paint, clay or words, that you can use to make something personal, strange or beautiful. The School for Poetic Computation, an artist-run school that started in New York in 2013, teaches code this way, more like creative writing than a job skill.

A website is a great place to start, because when you make one you get to be both the author and the architect. It doesn't have to be a résumé or a store. It can be a room, a shelf, a garden, a diary, a zine, or just a place to keep things so they don't disappear. Laurel Schwulst's essay My website is a shifting house next to a river of knowledge. What could yours be? is a really good way to start imagining what yours could be. She calls a website “an individual act of collective ambition.”

HTML is the oldest material on the web and also the most forgiving. You don't need to install anything, buy anything or ask anyone's permission to use it.

What we will learn

The hope is that after reading through and following this workshop, you'll be able to make a small website, keep it, and add to it a little at a time, until it becomes a living archive of whatever you'd like: your work, your days, the things you love. Just what you choose to keep. In the future we can learn how to host these files so anyone on the web can access them, but for now they can be passed around as files through message, email, flash drive, or any other way of sending files.

The first session

  1. Start by watching these two videos by Laurel Schwulst. They go over the basics of HTML and CSS.

  2. Open up the plain text editor that's already on your computer. You don't need to download anything.

    • Mac: TextEdit. Before you start, open its Settings. Under New Document, pick Plain text. Under Open and Save, check Display HTML files as HTML code instead of formatted text, and uncheck Add “.txt” extension to plain text files.
    • Windows: Notepad. When you save, set Save as type to All files so your file stays .html.

  3. Download the starter folder my-website.zip

    Unzip it and you'll have a folder called my-website with an index.html file inside. If you'd rather make it yourself, make a folder called my-website, and inside it save a new file called index.html with this code in it: 

    <!DOCTYPE html>
<html>
  <head>
    <meta charset="utf-8">
    <meta name="viewport"
          content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1">
    <title>my website</title>
  </head>
  <body>
    <h1>hello, i'm …</h1>
    <p>this is my website. it's a room i'm building.</p>
  </body>
</html>

  4. Open index.html in your web browser by dragging it onto a browser window or double-clicking it. That's your website. For now it only lives on your computer.

  5. Once you've watched the CSS video, try giving it some style. Put this inside <head>, right before </head>:

    <style>
  body {
    background: lavender;
    font-family: Georgia, serif;
    padding: 2em;
  }
</style>

  6. Now change one thing, save, and reload the browser. That loop of change, save, reload is most of what making websites is.

Adding sound and video

Songs, recordings, voice notes and videos go on your website the same way everything else does, as files in your folder.

1. Put the file in your folder

Copy the file into my-website, next to index.html. Give it a short name with no spaces or capital letters, like my-song.mp3, because names with spaces or capitals can work on your computer and then break once your site is online. MP3 (for sound) and MP4 (for video) play in every browser. Videos straight from your phone can be really big, so try exporting a smaller copy if you can.

2. Link to it

The simplest way is just a link. Clicking it opens the file in the browser's own player.

<a href="my-song.mp3">listen to my song</a>

3. Or put a player on the page

<audio src="my-song.mp3" controls></audio>

<video src="my-video.mp4" controls width="480"></video>

controls adds the play button and the timeline. Without it there's no way to press play. width sets how wide the video is in pixels. If you want a still picture to show before it plays, add poster="still.jpg".

If you want a video that plays by itself on repeat, like a moving picture:

<video src="loop.mp4" autoplay muted loop playsinline></video>

Browsers don't let sound start on its own, so a video can only play by itself if it's muted. playsinline stops iPhones from jumping into full screen.

4. If it's inside another folder

If you keep your sound and video in a folder inside my-website, like one called media, just put the folder's name in front: src="media/my-song.mp3".

This page does exactly that. Its soundscape and video live in its own media folder:

<audio src="media/rainbirdssoundscape.mp3"
       controls preload="none"></audio>
<video src="media/oregonoceanvideo.mp4"
       poster="media/oregonoceanvideo.jpg"
       controls playsinline preload="none"></video>

preload="none" means the file only downloads when someone presses play, which saves data on phones, so it's good to add to anything long. Until then, the video shows its poster, a still picture saved next to it.

Heads up: a video straight off your phone usually has the exact location where you filmed it hidden inside the file. This one was made smaller and had that removed before it went up. You should do the same with yours, just ask us how.

5. Video that's already on YouTube or Vimeo

Press Share, then Embed, and copy the code it gives you (it starts with <iframe). Then paste it into your page. If you see an option for privacy-enhanced mode, check it. The player comes from their website, not your folder, so if you open your page straight from your computer, YouTube might show Error 153 instead of the video. That's normal, and it'll play once your site is online.

Laurel's two videos at the top of The first session are embedded this way. Here's the code for the first one:

<iframe src="https://www.youtube-nocookie.com/embed/CkzbI1Tv_rQ"
        title="Basics of HTML, by Laurel Schwulst"
        allow="encrypted-media; picture-in-picture; fullscreen"
        allowfullscreen
        referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin">
</iframe>

Keeping it

Your website is the my-website folder, and everything is in it, your sound and video too. Keep it somewhere you'll find it again, and make a copy too: on a USB stick, your cloud drive, or just email it to yourself. Bring it with you every time.

This page is made the same way yours will be. On a computer, right-click it and choose View Page Source to see the HTML behind it. It's the same stuff you're about to write, just more of it.
